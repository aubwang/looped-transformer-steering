Building a Transformer on Eleven Words

I've looked around the internet and haven't really found a guide to transformers that felt satisfying from both a motivational and a mathematical perspective, so I set out to write my own. My goal here is to make it so that every component of the transformer arrives naturally as the solution to a concrete obstacle we run into, and also to express every step cleanly in math.

2 · Turning words into numbers

So counting works fine when a context appears verbatim in our data; we can say that when the cat sat on the appears, the next word is mat 50% of the time, hat 25% of the time, and grass 25% of the time.

OK, but what if someone asks us to predict what word comes after a cat sat on the ____?

Since none of our four sentences start that way, our percentage lookup table can't answer that question.

Changing the first word from the to a still leaves us looking for something a cat could sit on.

And if we swapped cat for dog, many of the same endings would still make sense.

As computers have no intuitive sense of language, we need to figure out a way we can represent these words as numbers. That way, the model can compare words with each other. Then our question becomes: how can we assign numerical representations to every word in our word universe , and how can we make it so that these numbers have some sort of semantic meaning?

Ideally, we want position offsets with three properties:

  • Offsets are small and bounded, so our word embeddings don't get drowned out as sentence length grows.
  • Distinct positions get distinct offsets, so that we can cleanly tell which position a word is in.
  • Stepping from one position to the next is consistent, so the spacing between two neighboring words stays the same, no matter where they are in the sentence.

5 · The problem with position-only weights

Squashing into already lets our trained model generalize across similar words in the same slot (swapping cat for dog at position , or the for a at position ). And notice that having "fixed parameters" after training isn't a bug by itself: once training finishes, every model's learned parameters stay fixed.

What holds our Section 4 model back is what those weights are attached to: they are position-only weights, storing one number per slot without looking at the word vector sitting there. That creates two big problems.

1. The model breaks when we shuffle or reword the sentence

Remember that we can interpret the learned weights as assigning slot greater or lesser importance: if , the model listens more to whatever word happens to sit in slot than in slot .

What if we ask our model to fill in the blank for dog on the grass chased _____?

Because is tied to the slot number, the cat sat on the ____ puts its largest weights on positions and (cat and sat), and dog on the grass chased _____ also puts its largest weights on positions and (on and the). Clearly on and the are not the words that tell you what the dog chased. Even if you keep the exact same words and shuffle the cat sat on the ____ into on the cat sat the ____, slot (the) still gets high weight.

Position-only weights generalize across words in the same slot, but they can't follow important words when they move to different slots.

Iterative generation exposes the following problems:

  • Rows we never trained don't exist. In our training data, we have sentences that are all words long (the cat sat on the mat/hat/grass), and therefore training only gives us the weights for (and is the one that predicts the blank in the cat sat on the ___). However, if we wanted to take this a step further and predict the cat sat on the mat ____ (or beyond), we need , but we can't compute it because it needs weights that weren't ever trained ().
  • Trained rows don't share what they learned. Each is only updated by examples where position reads position . This means that if our training teaches something like "after the, look back at the noun", that heuristic only gets used when position is exactly and it's evaluating position . Essentially we are forcing our weights to work in a very granular and compartmentalized manner, which is not good for language, where what matters is which words are nearby and how far back they are, not their absolute position. For example, if you read an article like a or the, you expect a noun like cat to follow the article. This doesn't change if you read the article at the start of a book or at the end of a book.
  • Way too many weights. A context of length needs separate weights. Doubling the context roughly quadruples the weights. If you want a context length of , you would need weights.
