Draft, second pass

When looped transformers settle, oscillate, and respond to a push

Experiments on how repeated computation changes a model's state, its next-token probabilities, and its response to interventions.

Introduction

A language model turns a text prefix into a probability distribution over the next token. A looped transformer spends more computation on that prediction by applying the same block of layers repeatedly before producing the distribution. This raises two questions: does the internal state settle as we add more loops, and what determines whether an intervention survives that repeated computation?

Huginn is a language model that applies the same block of layers over and over before it writes each token. How many times is a setting. Give it three worked sums and a fourth to finish:

33 + 36 = 69
415 + 419 = 834
3563 + 3209 = 6772
816 + 254 =

With 511 loops per token it writes 1070, which is right. With 512 it writes 1060. This is not noise that more loops would average out. At every loop count we checked, from 449 to 512, the model's first choice for the third digit was 7 when the count was odd and 6 when it was even.

The model's internal state alternates between two states, and in this example the two states disagree about the next digit. The example was found by a targeted search; it illustrates a possible consequence of oscillation, not how often ordinary prompts produce different answers.

This post develops two findings from experiments on Huginn and Recurrent-Llama:

  • Repeated computation can sustain an oscillation. On 200 fresh Huginn addition problems, 26 ended on a period-two orbit. In those cases, the oscillation began at an earlier position and propagated forward through attention. Damping the source position often stopped it. A change in the selected answer was rarer and required targeted searches to find.
  • The direction that changes a score immediately can differ from the direction that changes it after looping. Interventions that account for the loop moved target scores more than readout-gradient pushes in the experiments below. The score changes did not reliably make the target token become the answer.

First, I explain how a transformer produces a next-token distribution, including what attention and the MLP compute. Readers familiar with those components can skip to the oscillation results. The next two sections present the measurements and the mechanisms behind them. Interactive examples marked “toy” illustrate the mathematics; panels marked “real model” report measurements from trained models.

Background: from text to next-token probabilities

The task: a probability for every possible next token

Start with what a language model outputs. Text is split into tokens, items from a fixed list called the vocabulary V: whole words, parts of words, punctuation marks, digits. Real vocabularies have tens of thousands of tokens. This section uses a toy vocabulary of eleven words:

V = {the, a, on, cat, dog, bird, mat, roof, sat, chased, saw}

Given some text, the model has to say what comes next. It does not name a single token; it gives every token in V a probability. In the language of probability, the next token is the outcome of a random experiment, and V is its sample space: the list of possible outcomes. A probability distribution on V is one number per token, each at least 0, adding up to 1:

p(v | text) ≥ 0 for every v in V, and the p(v | text) add up to 1

The bar is read “given”: p(mat | the cat sat on the) is the probability that the next token is “mat”, given that the text so far is the cat sat on the. The opening example is one such list: for the prefix 816 + 254 = 10, Huginn gives a probability to every token in its vocabulary, and the replay in the next section shows just the two entries for 7 and 6.

Why two conditions are enough. Formally, a probability measure gives a number to every event, a subset A ⊆ V such as “the next word is an animal”. It must satisfy P(A) ≥ 0, P(V) = 1, and P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) whenever A and B do not overlap. On a finite sample space, the numbers for single tokens settle everything. Given p(v) ≥ 0 adding up to 1, set

P(A) = Σ_{v ∈ A} p(v)

A sum of non-negative numbers is non-negative; summing over all of V gives 1; and a finite sum over A ∪ B splits into the sum over A plus the sum over B. (Countable additivity adds nothing here: in a sequence of non-overlapping subsets of a finite set, all but finitely many are empty.) Conversely, every probability measure on V comes from its single-token values p(v) = P({v}). So checking a proposed distribution means checking two things: nothing negative, total 1.

What the right probabilities are: counting. Take a huge body of text and find every place where the cat sat on the occurs. Say it occurs 1,000 times: 700 times followed by “mat” and 300 times by “roof”. Picking one of those places at random and reading the next word is a random experiment with outcomes in V, and its probabilities are the frequencies:

p(mat | …) = 0.7, p(roof | …) = 0.3

and 0 for the other nine words. This passes the check. Write n_v for the number of places followed by token v, out of N places in all. Then p(v) = n_v/N ≥ 0, and since every place is followed by exactly one token, the n_v add up to N and the p(v) add up to 1. (A place at the very end of a text has no next token; real vocabularies include an end-of-text token so that it does.) Another way to see it: picking one of the N places uniformly at random is a probability measure on the places, and the map “place ↦ the token after it” carries it over to V as P(A) = #{places whose next token is in A}/N.

Counting only works for text that has been seen many times, though, and most text is new: a long prefix may never have appeared before. So a model cannot be a lookup table. It is a function that computes the eleven numbers from the text, with adjustable weights, and the weights are tuned so that on text where we do know what came next, its probabilities come as close to the counted frequencies as possible.

How “as close as possible” is measured. Training shows the model text one position at a time. At each position, the model gives its distribution, the text shows which token w* actually came next, and the model is charged a penalty, its loss:

ℓ = −log p(w* | text)

The loss is 0 if the model gave w* probability 1, and grows without limit as that probability falls to 0. Now suppose the model says p for “mat” and 1 − p for “roof” at all 1,000 places. It pays −log p at the 700 places followed by “mat” and −log(1 − p) at the 300 followed by “roof”. With f = 0.7, its average loss is

L(p) = −f log p − (1 − f) log(1 − p)

Setting the derivative to zero, −f/p + (1 − f)/(1 − p) = 0, gives p = f. The average loss is smallest exactly when the model's probability equals the counted frequency, and the same holds with any number of possible tokens. So lowering the loss on a large amount of text pushes the model's probabilities toward the frequencies; on new text, the model has to generalise from similar text it has seen.

Toy · two possible next words

The same with any number of tokens. Let f be the counted distribution and p any distribution with every p_v > 0. The average loss is L(p) = −Σ f_v log p_v, and using log x ≤ x − 1,

L(p) − L(f) ≥ 1 − Σ_{f_v > 0} p_v ≥ 0

Equality needs p_v = f_v for every token, since log x = x − 1 only at x = 1. So the counted distribution has the lowest average loss of all distributions on V, and it is the only one that does (this is Gibbs' inequality).

So the model's job is this: from the text, compute one number per vocabulary token, forming a probability distribution. A transformer does it in three stages. First, it turns each input token into a list of numbers and stacks those lists into a matrix. Second, a series of layers updates that matrix, letting every token take in its context. Third, it reads the last row of the result and turns it into the distribution. This section builds the stages in that order, with the toy vocabulary and the sentence the cat sat on the mat as a running example.

Embeddings: every vocabulary token gets a vector

A model computes with numbers, so the first step gives every token in the vocabulary a vector. The model stores an embedding table with one row per vocabulary token; looking a token up returns its row, the token's embedding. Write e_w for the embedding of token w. Every embedding has the same length d, which is a design choice of the model (d = 5,280 in Huginn). Training learns the numbers in the table, and it tends to put words that are used in similar ways near each other, because that helps predict the next word. The coordinates themselves have no preset meaning such as “animal” or “verb”; the meaning is in which words end up close together.

In the toy, d = 2, so each embedding is a point in the plane and the whole table can be drawn. The points are placed by hand to imitate what training does.

A sentence is still a bag of tokens

Now take the sentence the cat sat on the mat. Look up each of its six tokens and stack the embeddings as rows, in order:

the cat sat on the mat → six rows of embeddings

Rows 1 and 5 are identical, because both are “the”. Nothing in these numbers says which word came first: “the mat sat on the cat” gives the same six rows, stacked differently. As far as the numbers go, this is a bag of tokens, so each row needs to carry its position. There are two obvious ways to try.

Recording position: two tempting ideas

Try 1: just use the row number. The rows are already in order, so why not read the order off them? Because the computations that come later treat the rows as interchangeable: every row goes through the same operations, so shuffling the rows only shuffles the results. That is what lets one set of weights handle every position and any length of text, but it also makes the row number invisible. Position has to be written into the row's own numbers, so that it travels with the row.

Try 2: add a column that holds the position. The simplest way to do that is a third coordinate equal to 1, 2, 3, and so on:

six rows with a third column 1 to 6

Rows 1 and 5 now differ. But on longer texts the column fails in one of two opposite ways:

  • Crowding out. Left as 1, 2, 3, …, the column grows without limit. At position 1,000 it reads 1,000 beside word coordinates of about ±1, and the word becomes a rounding error.
  • Crowding in. Dividing by a maximum length, say 1,000, keeps the column below 1 but squeezes neighbours together: positions 500 and 501 become 0.500 and 0.501, which the model can hardly tell apart. (Dividing by the text's own length instead would make position 5 mean something different in every text.)

The fix: arrows around a circle

The failures say what a position vector needs: a fixed, modest size, so it never crowds out the word, and a clear difference between neighbours, so they never crowd in. One more wish: later steps compare rows with each other, so “the word one back” should look the same at position 7 and at position 7,000. Arrows turning around a circle give all three. Pick an angle ω and give position i the unit arrow at angle iω:

p_i = (cos iω, sin iω).

Every arrow has length 1, and neighbours are a full angle ω apart wherever they are in the text.

Turn one step, then compare. Turning an arrow by ω is multiplication by one fixed rotation matrix, and it moves every position onto the next one:

R p_j = p_{j+1}

To find the word just before position i, turn every position's arrow one step and see which one lands on p_i. For unit arrows the inner product is the cosine of the angle between them, so

p_iᵀ R p_j = cos((i − j − 1)ω)

which reaches its maximum, 1, exactly when j = i − 1. Because R is the same matrix at every position, one fixed matrix gives a “one word back” comparison that works anywhere in the text. Rotations are close to the only way to get this: if stepping forward is to be one fixed linear map, and the arrows must stay bounded however long the text runs, that map has to be a rotation.

The toy has room for one circle. It uses ω = 45° and shortens the arrows so they do not swamp the word coordinates:

p_i = 0.25ρ(cos iω, sin iω), ω = 45°

Here ρ is a size dial, the slider below. At its default, ρ = 0.6, position 1 gets p_1 ≈ (0.11, 0.11). Shortening every arrow by the same factor changes no angles, so turning and comparing works as before.

How finely to slice the circle: many hands

The step ω is chosen by hand, and with one circle every choice fails somewhere. Neighbours match by cos ω, so a small step makes them nearly identical: ω = 1° gives a match of 0.9998, crowding in again. A large step separates neighbours, but the arrow comes back around after 360°/ω steps, so distant positions collide. In the toy, with 45°, position 9 would land exactly on position 1.

Real encodings use many circles at once, each in its own pair of coordinates and each turning at its own speed, like the hands of a clock. The second hand separates neighbouring seconds but repeats every minute; the hour hand barely moves but tells morning from afternoon. Read together, the hands pin down the time. With speeds ω_1, ω_2, …,

p_i = (cos iω_1, sin iω_1, cos iω_2, sin iω_2, …), p_iᵀp_j = Σ_k cos((i − j)ω_k)

Each term is 1 only when its own hand lines up, so the sum reaches its maximum only when every hand lines up at once. A fast hand makes neighbours differ; a slow hand stops distant positions from colliding.

Toy · three hands, positions 1 to 200

One hand fails; three hands together work

The dials show the three hands at position i (black) and at a second position j (orange). The chart shows the match between p_i and every p_j, averaged over the hands in use, so a perfect match is 1. Point at the chart to choose j; it starts at the best look-alike at least two positions away.

The original transformer uses d/2 circles with speeds ω_k = 10000^{−2k/d} radians per step. The fastest turns 1 radian per step and comes around every 2π ≈ 6.3 tokens; the slowest takes about 63,000 tokens; the rest are spaced evenly on a log scale between, like the digits of a number. The only knob is the base, 10,000, and it is set by the longest text the model should handle: the slowest hand should not get all the way around within it. RoPE uses the same speeds inside attention. Llama 2 kept base 10,000; Llama 3 raised it to 500,000 for longer texts. One more detail: 1 radian is not a whole fraction of a turn, so these hands never return exactly to where they started. The slices do not need to divide the circle evenly.

GPT-2 does not use a formula: it learns a free vector for every position, starting from random numbers. Even so, its learned vectors, projected to three dimensions, trace a helix, a circle that also drifts steadily (“GPT-2's positional embedding matrix is a helix”, LessWrong, 2023). Training found the circle by itself. Huginn and Recurrent-Llama, the two models in this post, apply the rotation inside attention instead of adding offsets; the attention section shows how.

A sentence becomes the matrix X

The circle coordinates could be appended as extra columns, as in try 2, but models usually add them to the embedding, so every row keeps the same length d. Position i gets a position offset p_i with d coordinates, the same vector whichever token sits there. With thousands of coordinates, a trained model can keep word and position in mostly separate directions of the sum. Writing w_i for the token at position i, row i is

x_i = e_{w_i} + p_i.

Stacking the rows in order gives the matrix X ∈ ℝ^{n×d}, one row per position and one column per coordinate. For the running sentence at ρ = 0.6, in full:

X = embeddings + offsets, written out for the cat sat on the mat

The blue block depends only on the words: rows 1 and 5 are both “the”, so they match. The orange block depends only on the positions, and would be the same for any six-token text. Their sum separates the two copies of “the”: row 1 of X is (−0.89, 0.51) and row 5 is (−1.11, 0.29). A longer text adds rows, never columns. The figure builds X the same way for other sentences.

Where X goes next: rows that talk to each other

That finishes stage one: the text is now a matrix. Now look at what that matrix knows. Row 6 was built from two things only, the word “mat” and the number 6, so it says nothing about a cat or about sitting. Every row is like that: each one knows its own word and its own position, and nothing else. But what comes after the cat sat on the mat depends on the whole sentence. So before anything useful can be read off the last row, information has to travel between rows, and once it arrives, each row has to compute something with it.

That is the job of the layers. A layer takes X and returns a matrix of the same shape, n × d, still one row per position. It does not overwrite X; it adds a correction to it:

X ↦ X + ΔX

Think of X as a shared workspace. Each layer only writes what it has to contribute, and whatever earlier layers wrote stays available. Every layer makes two kinds of update, one after the other:

  • Attention moves information between rows: each row gathers from itself and the rows before it. This is the only step where positions talk to each other.
  • The MLP works within each row: it computes with whatever the row now holds, the same way for every row.

Both keep the shape, so one layer's output is a valid input to the next, and a model stacks many of them. After the last layer, the final row has taken in the whole sentence. Only then does stage three happen: a readout turns that row into one score per vocabulary token, and the scores become probabilities. The figure shows the whole pipeline; the next sections go through attention, the MLP and the readout, in that order.

Attention: what a head is, and what Q, K and V mean

Attention is the update that moves information between rows. To continue the cat sat on the mat, the last row needs to learn about the cat and the sitting; to predict the next digit of 816 + 254 = 10, information about earlier digits and carries has to reach the final position. One attention head computes a set of weights over the positions it can read, takes a weighted average of messages from them, and adds an update to the receiving row. Different heads can gather different information at the same time. A layer runs many of them in parallel—55 per layer in Huginn.

For an intuition, imagine a head learning to retrieve an earlier digit. The receiving position needs a way to ask for the relevant digit, each source position needs a way to say whether it matches, and a matching source needs a message to send back. These are three different jobs: query, key, and value. They are vectors computed from the current rows, using learned matrices; no one writes a literal instruction such as “find the tens digit” into the head.

Inside one head, each d-dimensional token vector is projected down to a much smaller scratch width k (k = 96 against d = 5,280 in Huginn) in three ways:

  • a query qi = WQ⊤xi ∈ ℝk: what position i is looking for;
  • a key kj = WK⊤xj ∈ ℝk: the label position j advertises to incoming queries;
  • a value vj = WV⊤xj ∈ ℝk: the k-number message position j sends if matched;

Every position produces all three: it can ask a question as a receiver and offer a key and value as a source. The matrices are shared across positions within this head, but different heads have their own matrices. Keys and values are separate because the information that makes a position relevant need not be the information we want to retrieve from it. A key might identify a digit's place in the sum, while its value carries information about the digit itself. This is an illustration of what a head could learn, not a claim about a particular head in Huginn.

A fourth matrix WO (k × d, whereas WQ, WK, WV are d × k) maps the received k-vector back into ℝd so every head writes back into the shared coordinates. Those four matrices combine into just two operations on X.

Whom does position i look at? Compare i's query with j's key by their inner product (divided by √k so scores stay moderate), and turn each row of scores into weights:

\begin{aligned}s_{ij}&=\frac{q_i^{\mathsf T}k_j}{\sqrt{k}}=x_i^{\mathsf T}B x_j,\\ A_{ij}&=\frac{\exp(s_{ij})}{\sum_{r\leq i}\exp(s_{ir})}\quad(j\leq i).\end{aligned}

where B = WQWK⊤ / √k is a bilinear form of rank at most k. Because a language model predicts left to right, position i may only look at positions j ≤ i (the causal mask), so Aij = 0 for j > i. Thus A(X) is an n × n lower-triangular row-stochastic matrix: the transition matrix of a Markov step backwards along the text. It depends on X, so who reads whom changes with the state.

Softmax: from scores to weights. The second line of the formula is called softmax. Scores are unrestricted numbers: they can be negative and need not add up to anything. Weights for an average must be positive and add up to one. So softmax exponentiates every score, which makes it positive, and divides by the total. For example, if position i can see three positions with scores 2, 1 and 0, exponentiating gives about 7.39, 2.72 and 1. The total is 11.11, so the weights are about 0.67, 0.24 and 0.09. Because e^{a+1} = e·e^a, a score that is larger by 1 always gets e ≈ 2.7 times the weight, whatever the other scores are. Why exponentiate, rather than simply divide each score by the total? Softmax turns up again at the very end, where the model turns scores into next-token probabilities, and the readout section works through that question with an example. Keep the two uses apart: this one decides where a head reads, the final one decides what the model might write next. Attention weights are an intermediate calculation, not probabilities of the answer.

Where position enters in modern models. The score s_ij above depends only on the vectors x_i and x_j, not on where they sit. Rotary position embeddings (RoPE), used by Huginn and Llama, put the positions in here, using the same circles as the position offsets. Before the inner product, the query of position i is rotated by an angle proportional to i, and the key of position j by an angle proportional to j. Write R_t for the rotation by t steps: it turns coordinates 1 and 2 by tφ_1, coordinates 3 and 4 by tφ_2, and so on, with fixed angles φ_1, φ_2, …. Because undoing a rotation by i steps and then rotating by j steps is a rotation by j − i steps, R_iᵀR_j = R_{j−i}, and

s_ij = (R_i q_i)ᵀ(R_j k_j)/√k = q_iᵀ R_{j−i} k_j/√k

The score now depends on the two vectors and on the distance j − i, but not on the absolute positions. “Two tokens back” is scored the same way anywhere in the text, and longer texts need no extra weights. This is also where two identical input rows, such as the two copies of “the”, first become different. The toy below uses the added offsets built earlier instead, so it has no rotation by position.

What does position i take from them? It averages the value vectors vj with weights Aij and writes the result back to ℝd through WO⊤. For example, weights 0.8 and 0.2 on two sources return the message 0.8v1 + 0.2v2; the output map turns that message into an update that can be added to xi. Across all rows at once (XWV has rows vj⊤), the head's output is

\begin{aligned}\Delta X&=A(X)(XW_V)W_O=A(X)XM,\\ M&=W_VW_O.\end{aligned}

A(X) multiplies on the left and mixes positions; M multiplies on the right and acts linearly on coordinates. Notice that WQ, WK only ever act through B and WV, WO only through M. Factoring them through ℝk forces both B and M to have rank at most k, which is how dozens of cheap heads fit in the budget of one full d × d matrix.

The MLP: positions do not talk, features interact

After attention, a position's vector contains its previous state plus messages gathered from the context. What should it do with that information? Suppose earlier heads have brought information about two digits into the same row. A useful next step might be to detect a combination of those digits and write a feature that later layers can use. The MLP supplies a learned nonlinear transformation for this kind of local computation. Attention itself is nonlinear too—its weights depend on the input—but once those weights are set, its message is a linear combination of the source values.

That is the job of the MLP (multilayer perceptron, an old neural-network name for a small two-layer map on vectors). The same fixed nonlinear map m : ℝd → ℝd is applied to every row xi separately, xi ↦ xi + m(xi), with zero traffic between positions:

m(x_i)=W_{\mathrm{out}}\,\sigma(W_{\mathrm{in}}x_i).

Read this formula from the inside out. First, Win mixes the coordinates of xi into a wider hidden space ℝp (typically three to four times d; in Huginn p = 17,920), σ : ℝ → ℝ applies a scalar switch to each coordinate on its own—classically max(0, u), which zeroes negative numbers and keeps positive ones, or a smooth version such as u / (1 + e−u)—and Wout maps ℝp back to ℝd.

The switch matters. Without σ, the two matrices would collapse to one linear map WoutWin; making the middle wider would not change that. With the switch, the update can depend on which combinations of input features are present. As a tiny invented example, suppose two coordinates a and b are either 0 or 1. A hidden unit computing max(0, a + b − 1) activates only when both are 1. Its output weight can then write an update for that combination. This example includes a bias (the −1); biases are omitted from the compact formula above.

Writing w1⊤, …, wp⊤ for the rows of Win and c1, …, cp for the columns of Wout turns m(xi) into a sum over p feature detectors:

m(x_i)=\sum_{r=1}^{p}\sigma(w_r^{\mathsf T}x_i)\,c_r.

Detector r measures a learned combination of coordinates, wr · xi. The activation σ turns that number into the coefficient of its write vector cr. With max(0, u), negative inputs contribute nothing and positive inputs contribute proportionally; smooth activations soften that cutoff. Sum all these writes to get the MLP's update. “Detector” is an intuition for the calculation: a hidden unit need not correspond to one clean, nameable concept.

The same MLP acts on every row separately, with the same weights. It can use context that attention has already brought into a row, but cannot read another row directly. The next layer's attention can then move the newly computed information elsewhere. Repeating this exchange—gather information, transform it locally—lets later layers work with features built by earlier ones.

Recent transformers, including Huginn and Llama, use a gated MLP, Wout(σ(W1xi) ⊙ W3xi), multiplying the switched coordinates entry by entry with a second linear view W3xi; the detector-and-write picture is the same.

Many heads, many layers

One full layer adds up its heads and then applies its MLP:

\begin{aligned}X&\mapsto X+\sum_h A_h(X)XM_h,\\ \text{then}\quad X&\mapsto X+m(X).\end{aligned}

In matrix terms, each attention update has a lower-triangular stochastic matrix on the left and a low-rank matrix on the right; the MLP then applies a nonlinear map to each row. I have omitted the normalisation used before each sub-step to control the scale of its input. An ordinary transformer composes L such layers, each with its own weights.

This left/right way of writing a head comes from Elhage and others, "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" (2021).

The readout: from the last row to next-token probabilities

That leaves stage three, which closes the loop back to the task we started with: one probability for every token in the vocabulary V, the eleven toy words. Say the text so far is the cat sat on the. Call the matrix after the last layer H. Its last row, h_5, belongs to the second “the”, and through attention it has taken in the whole prefix. From that one vector, the model must produce a probability for each of the eleven words. “Mat” or “roof” would be good guesses; “the” or “on” would be odd.

Scores. The readout stores one vector u_v ∈ ℝ^d for every vocabulary token v, and scores each token by an inner product, called its logit:

z_v = u_vᵀ h_5

A token scores high when the last row points along its vector and negative when it points away. In the toy, each token's readout vector is simply its embedding, u_v = e_v; GPT-2 does exactly this, and many other models learn a separate table. So for this prefix, the layers' job is to make h_5 point toward the “things” corner of the embedding plot. We have not built the layers, so for now set h_5 = (1.5, −0.6) by hand; in a trained model it comes out of the layers, whose weights training has tuned as described at the start of this section. Then z_mat = 1.77, z_roof = 1.75, and so on down to z_the = −1.74.

From scores to probabilities. Scores are not probabilities: some are negative, and they add up to 1.94, which means nothing. What should the conversion do?

  1. Valid probabilities. Every value at least 0, and all of them adding up to 1.
  2. Keep the order. A higher score gives a higher probability.
  3. Nothing impossible. Every token keeps some probability. Text does surprise, and a token given probability 0 that then occurs costs an infinite loss, −log 0.
  4. Room for confidence. The model should be able to say how sure it is, by spreading its scores further apart or keeping them close.

Try 1: divide each score by the total. The results add up to 1, but negative scores give negative probabilities: “the” gets −90%, while “mat” and “roof” get 91% each. And if the scores happen to add up to nearly 0, every result blows up.

Try 2: set negative scores to 0, then divide by the total. Now the values are valid: “mat” 26%, “roof” 26%, “dog” 19%. But “the”, “a”, “on” and “saw” get exactly 0, which breaks rule 3. Worse, doubling every score changes nothing, because the 2 cancels in the division, so the model has no way to be more or less sure (rule 4). And if every score is negative, it divides 0 by 0.

Softmax: exponentiate, then divide by the total. The function exp fixes all of this at once. e^z is positive for every z, never exactly 0, and grows with z; dividing by the total makes the results add up to 1:

p(v | input text) = exp(z_v) / Σ_u exp(z_u)

The left-hand side reads “the probability that v comes next, given this text”, and V is the vocabulary. It passes the check from the start of this section for any scores whatsoever. Each e^{z_v} is positive, so the denominator, a finite sum of positive numbers, is positive, and every p(v) > 0. The values add up to Σ_v e^{z_v} / Σ_u e^{z_u} = 1. So the model's output is a probability measure on V by construction, for every h_5 and every setting of the weights, trained or not; training only decides which one. The two failed recipes fail exactly this check: dividing by the total can go negative or divide by 0, and clipping divides 0 by 0 when every score is negative.

For confidence, compare two tokens: the total cancels, and

p(a)/p(b) = e^{z_a − z_b}

Only the gap between scores matters, and each extra unit of gap multiplies the odds by e ≈ 2.7. Doubling every score doubles the gaps and squares the odds, so a longer h_5 means a more confident model. Here softmax gives “mat” 25%, “roof” 25%, “dog” 15%, down to “the” at 0.8%: the model splits between the two words that fit, and nothing is ruled out. These are not the counted 70% and 30% from the start of the section, because h_5 was set by hand; training would move it, and the weights that produce it, until they came close. Doubling h_5 to (3, −1.2) raises “mat” and “roof” to about 37% and 36% and pushes “the” below 0.1%.

Repeated computation can sustain an oscillation

An ordinary transformer is a composition FL ∘ … ∘ F1 of different maps. A looped transformer keeps one block F and applies it repeatedly, feeding the encoded prompt e back in at every step:

x_{t+1}=F(x_t,e).

Here xt is the state after t loops. More loops mean more computation with no more weights. Huginn (Geiping and others, 2025) encodes the text through two initial layers to get e, loops a core block of four layers from a random starting state x0, and reads the answer off through two final layers.

Real model · Huginn

On 200 fresh addition problems, 26 (13%) did not settle but ended on an orbit of period two: the state alternated between two configurations. In each of the 26, the oscillation began at one earlier position and propagated to later positions through attention. Reducing the size of each update at the source position—a change called damping—stopped its oscillation in 24 of the 26 and stilled the whole text in 15.

Orbits in Huginn were first reported by Geiping and others (2025) and counted by Blayney and others (2026). The damping rule and its eigenvalue formula are in Movahedi and others (2026). What I have not found elsewhere is the source-position mechanism in a trained model.

How an earlier position can drive the oscillation

To explain these observations, consider how a small change to the state evolves over successive loops. In each of the 26 oscillating problems, the derivative at the source position had a real eigenvalue between −1.01 and −1.23. The significance of being below −1 is that a small displacement can reverse direction and grow on the next step.

The object is now a dynamical system. The first question is whether it settles to a fixed point x* = F(x*, e). Near x* everything is governed by the derivative DF(x*). Because causal attention is lower triangular, position i's update depends only on positions 1, …, i, so DF(x*) is block lower-triangular: each position has its own d × d derivative block Ji on the diagonal, and the eigenvalues of the whole system are simply the union of the eigenvalues at each position. If all of them lie inside the unit circle, the state settles. If a real eigenvalue at some position passes −1, the fixed point loses stability there and that position starts to alternate between two points—passing its oscillation forward through attention to later positions.

A persistent intervention must account for the loop

Suppose the state has settled at an attracting fixed point x* and you want to move the answer. The readout gives each candidate answer a score, with gradient g at x*: the direction in which that score rises fastest. The obvious move is to add a small vector P along g at the last position.

But the loop continues transforming the state after that push. I compare the readout gradient with a “lever” direction that accounts for those later transformations, and with a centroid direction pointing toward states associated with the target answer. Each intervention is added before every loop. The results come first; the calculation of the lever follows below.

Real models · Huginn and Recurrent-Llama, held-out problems

First model (Huginn). Unit directions were averaged over 20 training sums and tested on 20 held-out sums over 48 forced loops (ten single-digit candidates, push length 3). Why the immediate gradient can miss the effective direction

First, a one-off push does not last: because x* is attracting, a perturbation added on a single loop dies away as JtP → 0 (in Huginn, a one-shot answer edit shifted the score by +4.1 log-odds at once and washed back out to zero within 48 loops). To move where the state settles, add P before every loop, xt+1 = F(xt + P, e). Because causal attention only looks backwards, pushing the last position never alters any earlier position's state, keys or values, so the last position's own d × d derivative J governs the response on its own. To first order the score changes by

\begin{aligned}\text{Immediately:}\quad &g^{\mathsf T}P,\\ \text{After }T\text{ loops:}\quad &g^{\mathsf T}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}J^t\right)P.\end{aligned}

The best unit direction for the second quantity is not g. By Cauchy–Schwarz it is proportional to (J + … + JT)⊤g, which I will call the lever. For a symmetric J the two stay aligned. Nothing in a transformer forces J to be symmetric.

What these experiments establish—and what remains open

The oscillation experiments connect a source position's local instability to a change in the model's next-token probabilities, and show that damping that position can sometimes stop the oscillation. They do not estimate how often answers flip in general use: the answer-changing examples came from searches for near-ties, and the outcome also depends on the starting state.

The intervention experiments show why the response of the loop matters when choosing a direction to push. They measure target-score changes on the tested tasks and models. In Recurrent-Llama, none of the tested pushes made the target candidate become the answer, even though its score could increase. A change in True/False output preference likewise does not establish a change in an internal belief.

Together, the results make the dynamics between updates part of the explanation: to understand the effect of more computation or an intervention, we need to track how the state evolves. Whether these mechanisms are as useful on other tasks, architectures, and intervention sizes remains open.
