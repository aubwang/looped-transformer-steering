What happens inside a transformer that runs its layers in a loop: when it settles, when it does not, and which way it moves when you push it.

The two probabilities trade places because the model's internal state does not settle on this prompt. It ends up alternating between two states, and here the two states disagree about the digit. From start B the state alternates as well, on a slightly narrower orbit where 7 stays ahead at both phases (a multi-thousand-dimensional loop can support more than one period-two orbit on the same prompt, for instance when more than one earlier token oscillates). This example was found by a search built to find one, and I come back to how rare it is. First, why would one more loop change anything?

The rest of the post builds up to that question and one more. Section 1 is a fast tour of what a transformer computes, for readers who think in matrices and maps and have never looked inside one (skip to section 2 if you already know attention heads and MLPs). Section 2 applies the same layers in a loop and asks when the state settles. Section 3 asks which way the answer moves if you push on the looped state. Widgets marked "toy" run on small made-up matrices; boxes marked "real model" are measurements on trained models.

1. A transformer in five minutes

A text is first cut into tokens. Real models cut rarer words into pieces; here one word is one token. Each word of the vocabulary owns a fixed column vector in ℝd, learned in training, and so does each position in the text. The vector xi ∈ ℝd for position i is the sum of its word's vector and its position's vector. Stack the transposes x1⊤, …, xn⊤ as the rows of an n × d matrix X: one row per position in the text, one column per coordinate.

Grey dots are the words of the vocabulary. A numbered dot is one position of the sentence: its word's point, shifted by that position's offset. Point at a word, a dot or a row.

In a trained model, words used in similar ways end up near each other, because that helps predict the next word. Many current models put the position into the attention scores below instead of adding a vector; either way the two copies of "the" can be told apart.

Everything a transformer does from here has one form: keep X as a running workspace and add updates to it, X ↦ X + ΔX. Each layer makes two kinds of update with strictly separated jobs. First, attention lets positions read from earlier positions. Second, an MLP transforms each position's vector on its own.

Attention: what a head is, and what Q, K and V mean

At a single step, one position often needs several different pieces of earlier text at once: to finish 816 + 254 = 10, the last position needs the tens digits, whether the units carried, and how the worked sums were formatted. A single set of weights over the text is one probability distribution, so it mainly points at one place (or blurs several together). A layer therefore runs many small lookup units in parallel—55 per layer in Huginn—called attention heads, and adds all of their updates into X.

Inside one head, each d-dimensional token vector is projected down to a much smaller scratch width k (k = 96 against d = 5,280 in Huginn) in three ways, named after a database lookup:

  • a query qi = WQ⊤xi ∈ ℝk: what position i is looking for;
  • a key kj = WK⊤xj ∈ ℝk: the label position j advertises to incoming queries;
  • a value vj = WV⊤xj ∈ ℝk: the k-number message position j sends if matched;

and a fourth matrix WO (k × d, whereas WQ, WK, WV are d × k) maps the received k-vector back into ℝd so every head writes back into the shared coordinates. Those four matrices combine into just two operations on X.

Whom does position i look at? Compare i's query with j's key by their inner product (divided by √k so scores stay moderate), and turn each row of scores into probabilities:

sij = (qi · kj) / √k = xi⊤ B xj,    Aij = exp(sij) / Σr ≤ i exp(sir),

where B = WQWK⊤ / √k is a bilinear form of rank at most k. Because a language model predicts left to right, position i may only look at positions j ≤ i (the causal mask), so Aij = 0 for j > i. Thus A(X) is an n × n lower-triangular row-stochastic matrix: the transition matrix of a Markov step backwards along the text. It depends on X, so who reads whom changes with the state.

What does position i take from them? It averages the value vectors vj with weights Aij and writes the result back to ℝd through WO⊤. Across all rows at once (XWV has rows vj⊤), the head's output is

X ↦ A(X) (X WV) WO = A(X) X M,    M = WVWO.

A(X) multiplies on the left and mixes positions; M multiplies on the right and acts linearly on coordinates. Notice that WQ, WK only ever act through B and WV, WO only through M. Factoring them through ℝk forces both B and M to have rank at most k, which is how dozens of cheap heads fit in the budget of one full d × d matrix.

The MLP: positions do not talk, features interact

Once a head's weights A(X) are set, what lands at position i is only a linear combination of earlier vectors. Attention can copy a 6 and a 4 from earlier in the line into different coordinates of the vector at =, so both pieces of data now sit in the same row—but linear maps alone cannot compute nonlinear rules on them, such as deciding whether 6 + 4 carries.

That is the job of the MLP (multilayer perceptron, an old neural-network name for a small two-layer map on vectors). The same fixed nonlinear map m : ℝd → ℝd is applied to every row xi separately, xi ↦ xi + m(xi), with zero traffic between positions:

m(xi) = Wout σ(Win xi).

Here Win expands xi into a wider hidden space ℝp (typically three to four times d; in Huginn p = 17,920), σ : ℝ → ℝ applies a scalar switch to each coordinate on its own—classically max(0, u), which zeroes negative numbers and keeps positive ones, or a smooth version such as u / (1 + e−u)—and Wout maps ℝp back to ℝd.

Writing w1⊤, …, wp⊤ for the rows of Win and c1, …, cp for the columns of Wout turns m(xi) into a sum over p feature detectors:

m(xi) = Σr=1p σ(wr · xi) cr.

Detector r tests how far xi points along wr. When wr · xi is negative, σ stays near zero and detector r is silent; when it is positive, detector r fires and adds a multiple of its write vector cr into xi. Where attention looks up other positions in the text, the MLP looks up learned patterns inside xi, combining gathered features nonlinearly and injecting stored facts or arithmetic rules.

Recent transformers, including Huginn and Llama, use a gated MLP, Wout(σ(W1xi) ⊙ W3xi), multiplying the switched coordinates entry by entry with a second linear view W3xi; the detector-and-write picture is the same.

Many heads, many layers, and the readout

One full layer adds up its heads and then applies its MLP:

X ↦ X + Σh Ah(X) X Mh,   then   X ↦ X + m(X).

That is the whole machine: a sum of operators of the shape "lower-triangular stochastic matrix on the left, low-rank matrix on the right", followed by a row-wise nonlinear lookup. (Each row is also rescaled to a standard length before each sub-step so repeated additions stay bounded; I leave that out.) An ordinary transformer composes L such layers, each with its own weights, and finishes with a readout: a linear map takes the last row xn to one score (a logit) per word of the vocabulary, exponentials turn those scores into probabilities, and greedy decoding writes the word with the highest score.

This left/right way of writing a head comes from Elhage and others, "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" (2021).

2. Use the same layers again and again

An ordinary transformer is a composition FL ∘ … ∘ F1 of different maps. A looped transformer keeps one block F and applies it repeatedly, feeding the encoded prompt e back in at every step:

xt+1 = F(xt, e).

Here xt is the state after t loops. More loops mean more computation with no more weights. Huginn (Geiping and others, 2025) encodes the text through two initial layers to get e, loops a core block of four layers from a random starting state x0, and reads the answer off through two final layers.

The object is now a dynamical system. The first question is whether it settles to a fixed point x* = F(x*, e). Near x* everything is governed by the derivative DF(x*). Because causal attention is lower triangular (section 1), position i's update depends only on positions 1, …, i, so DF(x*) is block lower-triangular: each position has its own d × d derivative block Ji on the diagonal, and the eigenvalues of the whole system are simply the union of the eigenvalues at each position. If all of them lie inside the unit circle, the state settles. If a real eigenvalue at some position passes −1, the fixed point loses stability there and that position starts to alternate between two points—passing its oscillation forward through attention to later positions.

Real model · Huginn

On 200 fresh addition problems, 26 (13%) did not settle but ended on an orbit of period two. In each of the 26 the oscillation starts at one earlier position of the text, where the derivative of the loop has a real eigenvalue between −1.01 and −1.23, and attention passes it on to later positions. Damping that one position stopped its oscillation in 24 of the 26 and stilled the whole text in 15.

Does the oscillation change what the model says? Usually not. The two states of the orbit are close, so the output probabilities differ by a few percentage points, and the top choice changes only where two candidates were nearly tied to begin with. In the 44 oscillating prompts we first looked at, no answer digit changed. Two searches then went looking for near-ties. These are examples that a search was built to find, and they say nothing about how often this happens. The second search screened 400 new sums for nearly tied answer digits, ran the 85 closest for 512 loops, and found four answer digits flipping between the correct digit and a wrong one, out of 358 answer positions on the orbit. Every flip was reproduced by the model's own forward pass and in double precision. It also depends on where the loop starts: from one of four other starting states the 816 + 254 prompt never flips, and gives the correct digit at every loop count.

Orbits in Huginn were first reported by Geiping and others (2025) and counted by Blayney and others (2026). The damping rule and its eigenvalue formula are in Movahedi and others (2026). What I have not found elsewhere is the source-position mechanism in a trained model.

3. Now push

Suppose the state has settled at an attracting fixed point x* and you want to move the answer. The readout gives each candidate answer a score, with gradient g at x*: the direction in which that score rises fastest. The obvious move is to add a small vector P along g at the last position.

First, a one-off push does not last: because x* is attracting, a perturbation added on a single loop dies away as JtP → 0 (in Huginn, a one-shot answer edit shifted the score by +4.1 log-odds at once and washed back out to zero within 48 loops). To move where the state settles, add P before every loop, xt+1 = F(xt + P, e). Because causal attention only looks backwards, pushing the last position never alters any earlier position's state, keys or values, so the last position's own d × d derivative J governs the response on its own. To first order the score changes by

immediately:  g · P,     after T loops:  g · (J + J2 + … + JT) P.

The best unit direction for the second quantity is not g. By Cauchy–Schwarz it is proportional to (J + … + JT)⊤g, which I will call the lever. For a symmetric J the two stay aligned. Nothing in a transformer forces J to be symmetric.

With s = 0 a push along the gradient shows up at once and is gone after one loop, while a push at right angles does nothing at once and builds up loop by loop; slide s slightly negative and the lever even nudges the score backwards at once before turning positive on loop 1. The cosine tile is also the gradient push's final effect divided by the lever's, and the lever's immediate effect divided by the gradient's: one number, read three ways.

Real models · Huginn and Recurrent-Llama, held-out problems

First model (Huginn). Unit directions were averaged over 20 training sums and tested on 20 held-out sums over 48 forced loops (ten single-digit candidates, push length 3). The lever raised its pushed digit's centred score by +1.78 on average (189 of 200 problem–digit pairs positive), while the readout gradient (−0.05) and a centroid push towards the average state of that digit (−0.05) sat at random-direction noise (±0.05). As with negative s above, the lever started slightly negative at once (median −0.13) and turned positive after a single loop (+0.94). On True/False statements (60 held-out items, push length 12.5), the lever shifted True-against-False log-odds by a median 8.13, against 0.93 for the gradient and 0.25 for a linear probe direction separating true from false states—moving the output's affirmative polarity rather than an internal belief.

Second model (Recurrent-Llama-3.2). To check whether the pattern holds outside Huginn and across push sizes in double precision, the sum experiment was repeated on Recurrent-Llama-3.2 (McLeish and others, 2025)—an ordinary pretrained Llama model rebuilt so six of its layers loop around 32 times—using 40 training sums and 40 held-out sums (answers 70 to 79, each a single token). The catch. On Recurrent-Llama the correct answer leads the runner-up by a median of 4.84, about ten times the top of the chart above. No push made its target candidate become the model's answer, in 0 of 360 tries at every length (and at 9%, 5 of 360 pushes tipped the answer to a different, unpushed sum).

The formula and the lever are textbook calculus, used to train fixed-point models since Bai, Kolter and Koltun (2019). Wang and Reid (2026) already intervene along Jacobian-derived directions in Huginn and a related model. The measurements above are what I have not found elsewhere.
