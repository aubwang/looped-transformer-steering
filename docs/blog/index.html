What happens inside a transformer that runs its layers in a loop: when it settles, when it does not, and which way it moves when you push it.

The two probabilities trade places because the model's internal state does not settle on this prompt. It ends up alternating between two states, and here the two states disagree about the digit. From start B the state alternates as well, but 7 stays ahead, so nothing visible happens. This example was found by a search built to find one, and I come back to how rare it is. First, why would one more loop change anything?

The rest of the post builds up to that question and one more. Section 1 is a fast tour of what a transformer computes, for readers who think in matrices and maps and have never looked inside one. Section 2 applies the same layers in a loop and asks when the state settles. Section 3 asks which way the answer moves if you push on the looped state. Widgets marked "toy" run on small made-up matrices; boxes marked "real model" are measurements on trained models.

1. A transformer in five minutes

A text is first cut into tokens. Real models cut rarer words into pieces; here one word is one token. Each word of the vocabulary owns a fixed vector in ℝd, learned in training, and so does each position in the text. The vector for position i is the sum of the two: the vector of its word plus the vector of its position. Stack these as the rows of an n × d matrix X.

Toy · eleven words in the plane, placed by hand

From a sentence to the matrix X

Grey dots are the words of the vocabulary. A numbered dot is one position of the sentence: its word's point, shifted by that position's offset. Point at a word, a dot or a row.

In a trained model, words used in similar ways end up near each other, because that helps predict the next word. Many current models put the position into the attention scores below instead of adding a vector; either way the two copies of "the" can be told apart.

Everything a transformer does from here has one form: replace X by X plus something computed from X. There are two kinds of "something".

The MLP: positions do not talk

A fixed nonlinear map m : ℝd → ℝd is applied to every row separately. Row i becomes xi + m(xi). Nothing moves between positions.

Attention: positions talk, coordinates mostly do not

One attention head answers two questions with two matrices.

Whom does position i look at? A bilinear form B on ℝd scores every pair of positions, and each row of scores is turned into a probability vector:

sij = xi⊤ B xj,    Aij = exp(sij) / Σk exp(sik).

So A(X) is an n × n matrix with nonnegative entries whose rows sum to one: a row-stochastic matrix, the transition matrix of a Markov chain on the positions. It depends on X, which is where the nonlinearity lives. In a language model position i may only look at positions j ≤ i, so A is lower triangular.

What does it take from them? A linear map M on ℝd. The head's output is

X ↦ A(X) X M.

A multiplies on the left and mixes positions; M multiplies on the right and acts on coordinates. Row i of the output is a weighted average of the rows it looked at, passed through M.

Why four matrices, and why those shapes? In code a head has WQ, WK, WV (each d × k) and WO (k × d), with k much smaller than d. Only two products ever appear: B = WQWK⊤ and M = WVWO. Writing them as products of thin matrices is a rank constraint: each head gets a bilinear form and a linear map of rank at most k, and many cheap heads fit where one full-rank head would.

Pressing the same button again and again is also where section 2 starts.

Many heads, many layers

A layer adds up several heads and then applies the MLP:

X ↦ X + Σh Ah(X) X Mh,   then   X ↦ X + m(X).

That is the whole machine: a sum of operators of the shape "stochastic matrix on the left, low-rank matrix on the right", plus a pointwise nonlinearity. (Each row is also rescaled to a standard length before it is used; I leave that out.) An ordinary transformer composes L such layers, each with its own weights, and finishes with a readout: a linear map takes the last row to one score per word of the vocabulary, and the highest score is the answer.

This left/right way of writing a head comes from Elhage and others, "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" (2021).

2. Use the same layers again and again

An ordinary transformer is a composition FL ∘ … ∘ F1 of different maps. A looped transformer keeps one block F and applies it repeatedly, feeding the original text back in each time:

xt+1 = F(xt, e).

Here xt is the state after t loops and e is the embedded text. More loops mean more computation with no more weights. The model studied below, Huginn (Geiping and others, 2025), loops a block of four layers and reads the answer off through two further layers.

The object is now a dynamical system. The first question is whether it settles to a fixed point x* = F(x*, e). Near x* everything is governed by one matrix, the derivative J = DF(x*). If all eigenvalues of J lie inside the unit circle, the state settles. If a real eigenvalue passes −1, the fixed point survives but is no longer attracting, and the state starts to alternate between two points.

The map is x ↦ W tanh(x), with fixed point 0 and eigenvalues λ and 0.6. Click in the left panel to move the starting point.

Damping replaces the loop by x ↦ (1 − a)x + aF(x). It keeps the fixed point and moves each eigenvalue λ to 1 − a + aλ. Push λ below −1, then lower a until the orbit collapses.

Real model · Huginn

On 200 fresh addition problems, 26 (13%) did not settle but ended on an orbit of period two. In each of the 26 the oscillation starts at one earlier position of the text, where the derivative of the loop has a real eigenvalue between −1.01 and −1.23, and attention passes it on to later positions. Damping that one position stopped its oscillation in 24 of the 26 and stilled the whole text in 15.

Does the oscillation change what the model says? Usually not. The two states of the orbit are close, so the output probabilities differ by a few percentage points, and the top choice changes only where two candidates were nearly tied to begin with. In the 44 oscillating prompts we first looked at, no answer digit changed. Two searches then went looking for near-ties. These are examples that a search was built to find, and they say nothing about how often this happens. The second search screened 400 new sums for nearly tied answer digits, ran the 85 closest for 512 loops, and found four answer digits flipping between the correct digit and a wrong one, out of 358 answer positions on the orbit. Every flip was reproduced by the model's own forward pass and in double precision. It also depends on where the loop starts: from one of four other starting states the 816 + 254 prompt never flips, and gives the correct digit at every loop count.

Orbits in Huginn were first reported by Geiping and others (2025) and counted by Blayney and others (2026). The damping rule and its eigenvalue formula are in Movahedi and others (2026). What I have not found elsewhere is the source-position mechanism in a trained model.

3. Now push

Suppose the state has settled and you want to change the answer. The readout gives each candidate answer a score, and the score has a gradient g at x*: the direction in which the score rises fastest. The obvious move is to add a small vector P along g.

In a looped model the push has to survive the loop. Add P to the state before every loop, xt+1 = F(xt + P). To first order the score then changes by

immediately:  g · P,     after T loops:  g · (J + J2 + … + JT) P.

The best direction for the second quantity is not g. It is (J + … + JT)⊤g, which I will call the lever. For a symmetric J the two are close. Nothing forces J to be symmetric.

With s = 0 a push along the gradient shows up at once and is gone after one loop, while a push at right angles does nothing at once and builds up loop by loop. The cosine tile is also exactly the gradient push's final effect divided by the lever's: both equal the top-left entry of J + … + JT over the length of its first row.

The formula predicts the full table of score changes problem by problem: correlation 0.9997 at the smallest push, 0.976 at the largest, against 0.11 for the readout's own derivative with no loop.

The catch. The correct answer leads the runner-up by a median of 4.84 on these problems, about ten times the top of this chart. No push made its target the model's answer, in 0 of 360 tries at every length.

The formula and the lever are textbook calculus, used to train fixed-point models since Bai, Kolter and Koltun (2019). Wang and Reid (2026) already intervene along Jacobian-derived directions in Huginn and a related model. The measurements above are what I have not found elsewhere.
